# Universal Language History — Pre-Leibnizian & Contemporary Tradition

**Project:** Project Characteristica — Phase 1 Literature Review
**Date:** 2026-08-16
**Status:** In Progress

---

## Purpose

Reconstruct the intellectual environment that made Leibniz's project possible: the universal-language tradition before and during the seventeenth century, with special attention to Dalgarno and Wilkins, whose systems Leibniz read and critiqued.

---

## Part A: Pre-17th-Century Antecedents

### 1. The Cratylus Tradition (Plato, c. 360 BCE)
- **Text:** *Cratylus* — dialogue on whether names are conventional or natural
- **Contribution:** The question of natural vs. arbitrary signs; the ideal of names that reveal the nature of things
- **Legacy:** Every "real character" project inherits the Cratylist aspiration: a language whose signs are not arbitrary

### 2. The Adamic Language Doctrine (Medieval)
- **Claim:** Adam named the animals according to their natures (Genesis 2:19-20)
- **Theorists:** Augustine, Nicholas of Cusa, Paracelsus, Jacob Boehme
- **Contribution:** The theological warrant for a "language of nature"; pre-Babel unity of language
- **Legacy:** 17th-century projects often framed as recovering the Adamic language or constructing its equivalent

### 3. Ramon Llull (1232-1316)
- **Text:** *Ars Magna* (1305), *Ars Generalis Ultima*
- **Method:** Combinatorial "art" using 9 absolute principles (Goodness, Greatness, Eternity, Power, Wisdom, Will, Virtue, Truth, Glory) and 9 relative principles (Difference, Concordance, Contrariety, Beginning, Middle, End, Majority, Equality, Minority)
- **Mechanism:** Rotating concentric wheels (Lullian circles) mechanically combine principles into arguments
- **Purpose:** Universal method for converting non-believers via rational demonstration; also a logic of discovery
- **Legacy:** Direct ancestor of Leibniz's *ars combinatoria*; Leibniz explicitly acknowledged Llull's inspiration (while noting its defects)
- **Failure Mode:** Combinatorial generation without semantic control; arbitrary alphabet of principles; no truth criterion

### 4. Renaissance Mysticism & Cryptography
- **Trithemius** (1462-1516): *Steganographia* — angelic alphabets, cryptography
- **Agrippa** (1486-1535): *De Occulta Philosophia* — cabalistic letter combinations
- **Contribution:** Letter-combination methods; the idea that signs can encode knowledge
- **Legacy:** Combinatorial methods pass into magic and cryptography, then into philosophy of language

### 5. Francis Bacon (1561-1626)
- **Texts:** *The Advancement of Learning* (1605), *Novum Organum* (1620), *De Augmentis Scientiarum* (1623)
- **Contribution:** (a) The Great Instauration — systematic reform of knowledge; (b) critique of "Idols of the Marketplace" — the distorting influence of language on thought; (c) proposal for a "real character" — signs that represent notions directly, like Chinese characters or mathematical symbols
- **Legacy:** Bacon's critique of language as a source of error becomes a standard motivation for universal-language projects; his "real character" phrase is adopted by Wilkins

## Part B: The 17th-Century Universal Language Movement

### Context
The period 1620-1700 saw an explosion of universal language proposals, driven by:
- (a) Discovery of Chinese characters and Egyptian hieroglyphs (missionary reports, Athanasius Kircher)
- (b) The Baconian reform of science and the founding of scientific societies (Royal Society 1660, Académie des Sciences 1666)
- (c) The decline of Latin as international language
- (d) The success of algebra and mathematical symbolism as models of exact expression
- (e) The conviction that the diversity of languages (Babel) could be overcome by a rational construction

### 6. René Descartes (1596-1650)
- **Texts:** *Rules for the Direction of the Mind* (1628, pub. 1701), *Discourse on Method* (1637), letters to Mersenne (1629)
- **Idea of *mathesis universalis*:** A universal mathematics — the general science of order and measure, applicable to all objects of knowledge
- **Letter to Mersenne (Nov 20, 1629):** Descartes sketches and rejects a universal language: it would require a true enumeration of all human thoughts and their ordering; he thinks this is possible in principle but the requisite taxonomy of ideas does not exist; even if built, it would take a lifetime and be impractical
- **Key Distinction:** Descartes's *mathesis universalis* is a *method* of reasoning, not a *language*; he explicitly doubts the feasibility of the linguistic project
- **Legacy:** (a) Sets the agenda: any universal language requires an ordered inventory of ideas; (b) the Cartesian doubt about feasibility haunts all successors; (c) Leibniz's *scientia generalis* is partly a response to Descartes

### 7. Thomas Hobbes (1588-1679)
- **Texts:** *Leviathan* (1651), *De Corpore* (1655), *Computatio sive Logica* (Part I of De Corpore)
- **Contribution:** (a) Names as arbitrary marks and signs; (b) reasoning as computation: "reason is nothing but reckoning"; (c) truth as property of propositions, not things; (d) definitions as starting points of demonstration
- **Legacy:** Hobbes's "reasoning is computation" is a direct ancestor of the calculus ratiocinator; Leibniz cited Hobbes approvingly on the computability of reasoning while rejecting his nominalism
- **Limitation:** Hobbes's logic is syllogistic and nominalist; he has no constructive universal language

### 8. Jan Amos Comenius (1592-1670)
- **Texts:** *Via Lucis* (1641, pub. 1668), *Panorthosia* (1645-70), *Orbis Sensualium Pictus* (1658), *Panglottia* (planned)
- **Contribution:** (a) Pan sophism — universal wisdom; (b) a universal language ("panglottia") grounded in a complete ordering of things; (c) *Orbis Pictus* — the world pictured, a didactic encyclopedia of concepts with images; (d) pansophic dictionaries ordering all concepts
- **Legacy:** Comenius's pansophic ordering of all knowledge anticipates the encyclopedic classifications of Dalgarno/Wilkins; his influence on the Hartlib circle (London) fed directly into the Royal Society milieu where Wilkins worked
- **Failure Mode:** The complete pansophic encyclopedia was never produced; taxonomic ordering of concepts proved open-ended

### 9. Athanasius Kircher (1602-1680)
- **Texts:** *Polygraphia Nova* (1663), *Ars Magna Sciendi* (1669), *Turris Babel* (1679), *Oedipus Aegyptiacus* (1652-54)
- **Contribution:** (a) Polygraphy — a universal writing system of symbols (with an invented "universal" vocabulary for missionary use); (b) an ars magna sciendi reviving Lullian combinatorics; (c) claims about Egyptian hieroglyphs as a "real character" (based on his erroneous decipherment)
- **Legacy:** Kircher made the idea of a universal script widely known across Europe; his works were in Leibniz's intellectual environment (Leibniz corresponded with Kircher 1670-80)
- **Failure Mode:** His hieroglyphic claims were fantasy (hieroglyphs are phonetic, not a real character); his polygraphy was a word-substitution cipher, not a conceptual system

### 10. Francis Lodwick (1619-1694)
- **Texts:** *A Common Writing* (1647), *The Groundwork, or Foundation Laid (or So Intended) for the Framing of a New Perfect Language and an Universal or Common Writing* (1652), *An Essay Towards an Universal Alphabet* (1686)
- **Contribution:** (a) Earliest English universal writing scheme; (b) symbols designed from elementary strokes marking conceptual categories (similar in spirit to later characteristica); (c) one of the first systematic phonetic alphabets
- **Legacy:** Fellow of the Royal Society; member of the committee (with Wilkins) on universal language; directly influenced Wilkins's Essay
- **Failure Mode:** Primitive — his characters were too few and too unsystematic to express complex propositions

### 11. Cave Beck (1623-1706)
- **Text:** *The Universal Character* (1657)
- **Contribution:** A numeral-based universal language: 4,000 roots assigned numbers; grammar indicated by letters appended to numbers; an English-Latin dictionary of numbered roots
- **Method:** Real words replaced by arithmetic codes; grammar via affixes (e.g., verb forms via terminal letters)
- **Legacy:** Widely discussed in the Hartlib circle; an early example of the "characteristic numbers" idea (later central to Leibniz)
- **Failure Mode:** The root list is arbitrary and unprincipled; no ontology; grammar is ad hoc; no systematic semantics; memorization burden

### 12. George Dalgarno (c. 1616-1687) — *Ars Signorum, Vulgo Character Universalis et Lingua Philosophica* (1661)
- **Objective:** A universal character and philosophical language: words generated from a classification of things, so that the structure of the sign reveals the structure of the concept
- **Representation:** Symbols built from a small set of primitive marks; each mark encodes a category in the classification tree
- **Ontology:** A tree of genera: the top-level genera are (roughly): (1) substances (being), (2) accidents (attributes), (3) actions/relations, (4) grammatical/functional items (conjunctions, prepositions, etc.) — 21 chapters of categories in the Latin grammar sense
- **Method:** Root words carry the genus; additional marks encode species and differentia; word order and affixes encode grammar
- **Grammar:** Designed to be regular and transparent: the grammatical structure mirrors logical structure (subject-predicate; noun-verb correspondence)
- **Semantics:** Intensional — the meaning of a term is given by its position in the classification; words of the same genus share initial marks
- **Inference:** Syllogistic reasoning works because classification is tree-structured; containment relations are visible in the signs
- **Intended Users:** The learned; to replace Latin as international scholarly language
- **Strengths:** (a) First systematic published attempt to tie symbols to a conceptual classification; (b) transparent compositional morphology; (c) regular grammar
- **Weaknesses:** (a) The classification is ad hoc and philosophically naive (Aristotelian categories + improvisation); (b) 21 genera are arbitrary; (c) relations are poorly handled; (d) particles and logical constants are shoehorned in; (e) no account of truth, modality, or inference beyond syllogistic
- **Why It Failed:** (a) Epistemological: no principled basis for the classification of concepts; (b) linguistic: natural-language polysemy doesn't map onto the tree; (c) social: no community adopted it; (d) the tree is a finite snapshot of an open-ended conceptual space
- **Modern Relevance:** The "classification → symbol" pipeline is the prototype of every ontology-driven language; its failure isolates the ontology problem as the core difficulty

### 13. John Wilkins (1614-1672) — *An Essay Towards a Real Character and a Philosophical Language* (1668)
- **Objective:** (a) A "real character" — a written symbol system where each symbol denotes a thing/concept directly, independent of any spoken language; (b) a "philosophical language" — a spoken language whose words are generated from the same classification; (c) a universal standard for scientific communication, sponsored by the Royal Society
- **Ontology:** The famous 40 genera tree: (1) transcendental relations (e.g., being, quantity, quality); (2) substances (God, world, elements, stones, metals, herbs, trees, animals...); (3) accidents/qualities (magnitudes, powers, habits...); (40) miscellaneous (particles, exclamations, affixes). Each genus subdivided into species and differences — a hierarchical classification intended to cover "all things and notions"
- **Representation:** Real character: each genus has a distinctive radical glyph (e.g., a hook for "transcendental"); species and differences marked by appended strokes; the spoken philosophical language assigns consonant-vowel patterns to the same tree nodes (e.g., "de" = element, "deb" = fire, "deba" = flame)
- **Grammar:** Synthetic grammar in Part III: particles (the "transcendental" genus includes pronouns, prepositions, conjunctions as "transcendental particles"); regular inflection; word order mirrors thought-order
- **Semantics:** Positional: meaning = location in the tree; the system is designed so that knowing the classification gives you the meaning of any symbol
- **Inference:** Not a logic; Wilkins provides no inference rules — it is a *language*, not a *calculus* (a crucial contrast with Leibniz)
- **Intended Users:** The scientific community (Royal Society), international communication; not everyday use
- **Strengths:** (a) Monumental systematicity — 40 genera, ~2,030 species; (b) first complete published philosophical language with dictionary and grammar; (c) transparent compositionality of signs; (d) real character truly independent of phonology; (e) inspired by and grounded in contemporary natural history (Ray, Willughby contributed taxonomy)
- **Weaknesses:** (a) The ontology is philosophically indefensible at the margins: arbitrary genus boundaries; (b) the tree is a classification of *English/European* concepts — covert linguistic imperialism; (c) polysemy and metaphor break the one-sign-one-concept mapping; (d) no treatment of truth, validity, or inference; (e) the grammar is a rationalized Latin grammar; (f) the real character was hard to learn and write; (g) classification of living things (herbs, animals) was already obsolete when published
- **Contemporary Criticism:** (a) Robert Hooke: too cumbersome; (b) John Wallis: the classification is arbitrary; a real character must be grounded in actual philosophy; (c) Leibniz (in letters to Oldenburg 1670s): the enumeration of concepts is incomplete; Wilkins lacks the calculus — he gives a language but no method of reasoning; Leibniz: "I do not approve of his method of classification... it is not the words but the characters that must be real" (paraphrase); (d) later historians (Eco, Slaughter, Lewis, Subbiondo): the Essay is the apex of the movement but demonstrates the impossibility of a fixed taxonomy of all thought
- **Why It Failed:** (a) Epistemological: no principled ontology; the classification of all things is open-ended; (b) linguistic: natural language semantics (polysemy, context-dependence, metaphor) resists tree-encoding; (c) cognitive: the learning burden was enormous; (d) social: no institution sustained adoption; the Royal Society's sponsorship didn't produce users; (e) the rise of modern linguistics and the 19th-century historical-comparative paradigm made the project seem naive
- **Modern Relevance:** (a) The direct ancestor of controlled natural languages and ontology engineering (its genus tree = a top-level ontology); (b) its failure is the canonical demonstration that "language without calculus" is insufficient; (c) its real character is the ancestor of ideographic symbol systems and icons

### 14. Seth Ward (1617-1689)
- **Text:** *Vindiciae Academiarum* (1654, with John Wilkins)
- **Contribution:** (a) Advocated for a universal character and philosophical language as part of scientific reform; (b) defended the Baconian program at Oxford; (c) argued that a universal language would fix the "confusion of tongues" and enable exact science
- **Legacy:** With Wilkins, provided the institutional and intellectual framework at Oxford (the "1650s Oxford circle") that produced the Essay

### 15. Others in the Movement
- **Johann Joachim Becher** (1635-1682): *Character, pro Notitia Linguarum Universali* (1661) — a 2,000-root philosophical language; influenced by Comenius
- **Thomas Urquhart** (1611-1660): *Logopandecteision* (1653) — eccentric universal language proposal
- **Philipp von Zesen** (1619-1689): German universal language attempts
- **Gottfried Wilhelm Leibniz** (1646-1716): See LEIBNIZ_PRIMARY_SOURCE_MAP.md — read Wilkins/Dalgarno, and transformed the tradition from *language* to *calculus + characteristica*

## Part D: Key Architectural Lessons for Project Characteristica

1. **The ontology problem is the load-bearing wall.** Every failure traces to an indefensible or incomplete classification of concepts. A modern system cannot presuppose a fixed taxonomy.

2. **Language without calculus is inert.** Wilkins produced a language but no inference mechanism. Leibniz's contribution was to insist on the *calculus* side. Any successor needs both.

3. **One-sign-one-concept mapping breaks on polysemy, metaphor, context.** The tree-encoding of semantics is the core failure. Modern systems must represent ambiguity or handle context explicitly.

4. **Covert linguistic imperialism.** The "philosophical" classifications were classifications of European conceptual schemes. Language neutrality is a genuine open problem, not a solved one.

5. **Social adoption matters.** Even a technically superior system (Wilkins's was the best of its time) fails without users, institutions, and reasons to switch.

6. **Classification is open-ended; operations may not be.** If universality belongs to *operations* (composition, inference, transformation) rather than *vocabulary*, the project escapes the taxonomy trap. (Hypothesis to be developed.)

7. **The symbol-encoding choice is downstream.** Whether signs are numbers (Beck, Leibniz), strokes (Dalgarno), glyphs (Wilkins) is cosmetic; the semantics and ontology are what decide success.
